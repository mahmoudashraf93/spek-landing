The French travel phrases to learn first are **bonjour**, **merci**, **s'il vous plaît**, **excusez-moi**, **parlez-vous anglais ?**, and **je ne comprends pas**. For a café visit, add **un café, s'il vous plaît** and **l'addition, s'il vous plaît**. Learn a short exchange you can use, then keep the written phrases nearby for everything else.

This guide focuses on everyday requests in France. It gives you a way to begin an interaction and recover when you cannot follow the answer, without needing to memorise a whole conversation.

<h2 id="greetings">Open with a greeting</h2>

| English | French |
| --- | --- |
| Hello / good day | Bonjour |
| Good evening | Bonsoir |
| Please | S'il vous plaît |
| Thank you | Merci |
| Excuse me | Excusez-moi |
| Do you speak English? | Parlez-vous anglais ? |
| I don't understand | Je ne comprends pas |
| Goodbye | Au revoir |

Start your request with **bonjour** or **bonsoir**, then ask your question. **S'il vous plaît** and **parlez-vous anglais ?** use **vous**, a useful polite form when speaking to someone you do not know.

For a rough starting point, **bonjour** sounds like “bohn-ZHOOR” and **merci** like “mehr-SEE.” These are approximate English hints; French vowels and the **r** need listening practice. Use the linked learning resources to hear examples instead of treating an English spelling as an exact pronunciation.

<h2 id="cafe">Order, read the menu, and ask for the bill</h2>

| What you need | French |
| --- | --- |
| A table for two, please | Une table pour deux, s'il vous plaît |
| A coffee, please | Un café, s'il vous plaît |
| A carafe of water, please | Une carafe d'eau, s'il vous plaît |
| The bill, please | L'addition, s'il vous plaît |
| Can I pay by card? | Puis-je payer par carte ? |

Keep **l'addition** in your café vocabulary: it means the bill. For dishes, look for **la carte**, **le menu**, **le plat** and **le dessert**. [TV5MONDE's café vocabulary](https://apprendre.tv5monde.com/fr/aides/vocabulaire-les-commandes-au-cafe-au-restaurant) includes audio for these words.

A **menu** may describe a set meal, while **la carte** lists dishes you can choose individually. Read the price and what is included before choosing. In French restaurant wording, an **entrée** is a starter; do not assume the English-language use of “entrée” as a main course applies.

When ordering, point to the actual dish and state your quantity. That makes the item clearer than reading a name you have only just encountered. <h2 id="signs">Ask for directions and recognise basic signs</h2>

| English | French |
| --- | --- |
| Where is the station? | Où est la gare ? |
| Where are the toilets? | Où sont les toilettes ? |
| One ticket to Lyon, please | Un billet pour Lyon, s'il vous plaît |
| Entrance / exit | Entrée / sortie |
| Open / closed | Ouvert / fermé |
| Right / left | Droite / gauche |

Notice how **entrée** changes with context: on a building it means entrance; on a restaurant menu it can mean starter. Read the surrounding words before deciding which meaning fits.

For a train journey, keep the exact station name and train number visible. Asking for “the station” only helps if both people mean the same place. At a ticket counter, show your destination, date and booking details as you ask the question.

<h2 id="replies">What to say when the answer is too fast</h2>

Use **plus lentement, s'il vous plaît** for “more slowly, please,” or **pouvez-vous répéter ?** for “could you repeat?” Ask one short question at a time, and leave room for the answer.

If the difficulty is a number, look for the displayed price, departure time or platform. If it is a place name, show the spelling. The useful next step is to establish the detail you need, rather than translating a whole paragraph immediately.

Practise this point before leaving: say your greeting, ask a question, then open the written version of “I don't understand.” It makes the fallback part of the routine instead of something you have to invent under pressure.
