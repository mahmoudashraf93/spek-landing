For a trip to Egypt, start with **ahlan** (“hello”), **shukran** (“thank you”), **law samaht** (“please / excuse me,” to a man), **bikaam da?** (“how much is this?”), and **ana mish faahim / faahma** (“I don't understand”). Keep the Arabic text alongside the reading aid so you can show it when pronunciation or listening gets difficult.

<h2 id="masri">Use everyday Egyptian Arabic for everyday exchanges</h2>

**Egyptian Arabic**, often called **Masri**, is the everyday spoken variety this guide focuses on. It differs from Modern Standard Arabic, which you will encounter in formal writing and other contexts. A general Arabic translation is not automatically a natural Egyptian conversation phrase.

Egyptian speech also varies within Egypt. This is a small starter kit, not a claim that everyone uses identical pronunciation or wording. Latin-letter spellings are reading aids: you may see the same sound written several ways in different resources.

<h2 id="greetings">Learn a greeting and the reply</h2>

| English | Arabic | Reading aid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hello, informal | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">أهلاً</span> | Ahlan |
| Good morning | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">صباح الخير</span> | Sabaah el-kheir |
| Reply to good morning | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">صباح النور</span> | Sabaah en-noor |
| Thank you | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">شكرًا</span> | Shukran |
| No, thank you | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">لا شكرًا</span> | La', shukran |
| Goodbye | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">مع السلامة</span> | Ma'a s-salaama |

Practise the greeting and reply as a pair. That gives you something to recognise when the other person responds. **Kh** in the reading aid represents a sound that English spelling does not capture well; listen to a recording rather than treating “kheir” as an ordinary English word.

Use the text as something you can show as well as say. Preserve the Arabic letters when copying it into a note, and keep the English meaning on the same line so you can find the right phrase quickly.

<h2 id="requests">Short requests for prices and everyday help</h2>

| English | Arabic | Reading aid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Please / excuse me, to a man | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">لو سمحت</span> | Law samaht |
| Please / excuse me, to a woman | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">لو سمحتي</span> | Law samahti |
| How much is this? | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">بكام ده؟</span> | Bikaam da? |
| Where is the bathroom? | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">الحمام فين؟</span> | El-hammaam fein? |
| I don't understand, male speaker | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">أنا مش فاهم</span> | Ana mish faahim |
| I don't understand, female speaker | <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">أنا مش فاهمة</span> | Ana mish faahma |

Point to the item while asking **bikaam da?** Then confirm the amount in writing or on a display if you do not recognise the spoken number. Keep the quantity clear: one item, several items and a price per unit are different requests.

**Fein** means “where.” For a destination, show the actual address while asking for help rather than replacing a place name with a guessed pronunciation. Save the hotel name and street in Arabic from the accommodation's own information where available.

The [Kalimah vocabulary guide](https://kalimah-center.com/egyptian-arabic-vocabulary/) includes everyday requests and examples. [Omniglot's Egyptian phrase collection](https://www.omniglot.com/language/phrases/arabic_egyptian.php) provides another reference with audio links. Avoid assuming that a Latin-letter transcription alone supplies the rhythm and consonants.

<h2 id="gender">Check whether a phrase changes with the speaker or listener</h2>

There are two different kinds of change in the table. **Faahim / faahma** describes the person speaking: the version depends on who is saying “I don't understand.” **Samaht / samahti** addresses the person listening: the version depends on whom you are asking.

Keep these labels when saving your phrase card. Removing them can make two alternatives look interchangeable. Start with the forms that fit the exchange you are practising, and learn the other version alongside them.

Do not build a long sentence by swapping unfamiliar endings from different examples. Show a short complete phrase, then let the person respond. For a longer request, a teacher or fluent speaker can help you check wording and pronunciation in context.

<h2 id="backup">Prepare your first conversation and a written fallback</h2>

Choose a greeting, one question and a clarification phrase. Practise that three-step sequence with your real destination or purchase in mind. Save the Arabic address and a copy of the booking separately so they are easy to show.
