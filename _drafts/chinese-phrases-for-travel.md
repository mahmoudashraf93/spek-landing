For a trip to mainland China, start with Mandarin **nǐ hǎo** (“hello”), **xièxie** (“thank you”), **qǐngwèn…** (“excuse me, may I ask…”), and **duōshao qián?** (“how much is it?”). Save the Chinese characters as well as the pinyin. Showing the written phrase or destination is a useful fallback when tones or a fast reply get in the way.

<h2 id="scope">What this Chinese phrase kit covers</h2>

This guide uses **Mandarin** and **simplified Chinese characters**, with mainland-China travel in mind. “Chinese” does not identify one interchangeable spoken language for every destination. A Mandarin phrase list is not a Cantonese phrase list, and simplified or traditional writing is a separate choice from the spoken variety.

**Pinyin** writes Mandarin pronunciation using Latin letters and tone marks. The marks in **nǐ hǎo** carry pronunciation information; they are not decorative accents. Listen to a recording and practise the whole phrase rather than reading the letters as English. Dictionary spellings may not show every tone change in connected speech.

<h2 id="essentials">Start with greetings and clarification</h2>

| English | Chinese | Pinyin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hello | <span lang="zh-Hans">你好</span> | Nǐ hǎo |
| Thank you | <span lang="zh-Hans">谢谢</span> | Xièxie |
| Excuse me, may I ask… | <span lang="zh-Hans">请问…</span> | Qǐngwèn… |
| Goodbye | <span lang="zh-Hans">再见</span> | Zàijiàn |
| I don't understand what you're saying | <span lang="zh-Hans">我听不懂</span> | Wǒ tīng bu dǒng |
| Do you speak English? | <span lang="zh-Hans">你会说英语吗？</span> | Nǐ huì shuō Yīngyǔ ma? |

Use **qǐngwèn** before a question when getting someone's attention. If the reply is beyond what you understand, show the written information and restart with one detail. You do not need to keep speaking Mandarin after establishing that English or written text will work better for the exchange.

Keep the characters when copying these phrases. A pinyin-only note helps you attempt pronunciation; a note with characters also gives the other person something to read.

<h2 id="requests">Food, prices, and a clear item to point to</h2>

| English | Chinese | Pinyin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How much is it? | <span lang="zh-Hans">多少钱？</span> | Duōshao qián? |
| Do you have an English menu? | <span lang="zh-Hans">有英文菜单吗？</span> | Yǒu Yīngwén càidān ma? |
| I want this one | <span lang="zh-Hans">我要这个</span> | Wǒ yào zhège |
| Can I pay by card? | <span lang="zh-Hans">可以刷卡吗？</span> | Kěyǐ shuā kǎ ma? |

Point to the actual dish or item when using **zhège**, “this.” Check the quantity, size and amount separately. For a price, looking at the display is a useful way to confirm a number you did not recognise by ear.

Asking whether you can pay by card establishes a question, not an acceptance guarantee for your particular card. Confirm your payment method with the business. Payment setup and current provider requirements are separate travel preparation tasks.

For a menu without English, you can use our [menu translation guide]({{ '/blog/translate-menu-iphone/' | relative_url }}) to understand unfamiliar text. Keep the original dish name visible while asking staff about it.

<h2 id="destinations">Show the exact address rather than guessing a name</h2>

Save your accommodation's name and address in Chinese from its official booking information. Keep it available to show in a large, readable note, alongside the English version. Do the same for your first station or meeting place.

Useful labels to recognise are **地铁站 — dìtiězhàn** (“subway station”), **火车站 — huǒchēzhàn** (“train station”), and **机场 — jīchǎng** (“airport”). They identify a type of place, not your particular destination. Add the exact name before asking for directions.

For a hotel, keep the booking reference and property name together. If you ask someone for help, show only the information needed for that request rather than searching through a long list of phrases and unrelated bookings.

<h2 id="practise">Practise the first exchange and prepare for connectivity gaps</h2>

Choose a greeting, a question and a clarification phrase. Listen to Mandarin audio, repeat the complete line, then practise showing its characters. The [teacher's travel reference](https://www.macalaoshi.com/en/guides/chinese-phrases-for-travel) gives further examples and explains pinyin tone changes.

Save the phrase card and Chinese addresses where you can open them without a connection. Test any app and connection you plan to use after arrival; this guide does not claim that a particular online service will be reachable on every network in China.
