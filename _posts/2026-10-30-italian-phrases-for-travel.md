For a first trip to Italy, learn **buongiorno**, **grazie**, **scusi**, **per favore**, **parla inglese?**, and **non capisco**. Then add the phrases for your actual plans: a café order, a restaurant bill, and a question about the train platform.

You do not need to memorise a long list before leaving. Pick a few lines you can say, and keep the rest visible on your phone. A written phrase is useful when a busy station or café makes listening difficult.

<h2 id="start-here">Six Italian phrases to learn first</h2>

The hints below use capitals for the stressed syllable. They are approximate: English spelling cannot reproduce every Italian sound. Say the final vowels clearly rather than swallowing them.

| What you mean | Italian | Rough pronunciation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hello / good morning | Buongiorno | bwon-JOR-no |
| Thank you | Grazie | GRAHT-see-eh |
| Excuse me, politely | Scusi | SKOO-zee |
| Please | Per favore | pehr fah-VOH-reh |
| Do you speak English? | Parla inglese? | PAR-lah een-GLEH-zeh |
| I don't understand | Non capisco | non kah-PEE-skoh |

**Buongiorno** is a useful opening with someone you do not know. Use **buonasera** in the evening and **arrivederci** when leaving. **Ciao** is informal; you can save it for a more familiar exchange. Treccani distinguishes these [formal and informal greetings](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/formule-di-saluto_%28La-grammatica-italiana%29/).

A workable first exchange is simply: “Buongiorno. Parla inglese?” Pause for the reply before adding another question. The greeting gives you a natural start, and the language question establishes how the rest of the conversation will work.

<h2 id="cafe-and-restaurant">Order, ask for a table, and pay</h2>

Use **vorrei** — “I would like” — as a starting point for an order. Pointing to the item on a menu can be clearer than trying to pronounce an unfamiliar dish from memory.

| Situation | Italian | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask for two seats | Avete un tavolo per due? | Do you have a table for two? |
| Ask for the menu | Potrei vedere il menù? | May I see the menu? |
| Start an order | Vorrei… | I would like… |
| Order two coffees | Due caffè, per favore. | Two coffees, please. |
| Request the bill | Il conto, per favore. | The bill, please. |
| Ask the total | Quanto viene? | How much does it come to? |

Learn your order as one complete line. “Due caffè, per favore” is easier to retrieve under pressure than assembling separate words for two, coffee, and please. If you are ordering for one person, change **due** to **un**: “Un caffè, per favore.”

Before paying, look at the amount or ask the person to show it. Hearing a number correctly is a separate skill from asking the question correctly. You can confirm a displayed total without trying to learn every Italian number on the spot.

For an unfamiliar menu, use our [guide to translating a menu on iPhone]({{ '/blog/translate-menu-iphone/' | relative_url }}). A translation can help you understand a description; discuss dietary requirements directly with staff rather than treating a translated dish name as confirmation about ingredients.

<h2 id="trains-and-directions">Find your train and recognise the signs</h2>

Keep the **full station name**, train number, and destination from your booking visible. A city name alone may leave you choosing between several stations. Match the train number on your ticket to the departure board before looking for the platform.

| Italian on a sign or ticket | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Partenze / arrivi | Departures / arrivals |
| Binario | Track / platform |
| Carrozza | Coach / carriage |
| Posto | Seat |
| Ritardo | Delay |
| Cancellato | Cancelled |

These words help you read information, rather than just request it. For example, **carrozza 5, posto 8** identifies coach 5 and seat 8. Keep checking the departure board for the platform; do not assume an early display will remain unchanged. Italo explains this [station vocabulary](https://blog.italotreno.com/en/train-world/how-to-to-italy/) in its traveller guide.

| What you need | Italian |
| --- | --- |
| Which platform does my train leave from? | Da quale binario parte il mio treno? |
| Where is the main station? | Dov'è la stazione centrale? |
| Is the train on time? | Il treno è in orario? |
| Where is the bathroom? | Dov'è il bagno? |

Show your ticket while asking about the platform. The person can then identify your particular service. Follow the ticket provider's current instructions for validation or check-in; the word **biglietto** means “ticket,” but knowing the word does not tell you which rules apply to your fare.

<h2 id="understand-the-answer">Make the answer easier to follow</h2>

Start with one question at a time. If the answer is too quick, say **non capisco**, then show the phrase or your booking. Keep names and numbers available to point to: a station written in your notes is easier to verify than a place name heard once in a crowd.

Recognise **sì** (“yes”), **no** (“no”), **destra** (“right”), and **sinistra** (“left”). For numbers, practise **uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque** — one through five — with audio before your trip. Those first numbers help with quantities; a displayed price is still a better fallback for a total you did not catch.

Practise a short sequence rather than the whole page: greet, ask, listen, confirm. Try ordering one coffee, then imagine the person answering with a question you do not understand. Knowing how to stop and clarify is part of the phrase kit.
